How to Start a Task When You Have ADHD: 7 Techniques

By Jack Wallner · October 12, 2026

A practical way to start a task when you are stuck is to make the first step smaller than feels reasonable: open the file, put on one sock, run water in the sink. NIMH and CHADD both advise breaking large tasks into smaller steps. Techniques that add structure around that first step, such as if-then plans, short timers and body doubling, can also help. If starting is hurting your daily life, talk to a health care provider.

General information, not medical advice. Next Cue supports personal organization and does not diagnose, treat or manage ADHD or any other condition. If you think ADHD may be affecting your life, start with a health care provider.

Why starting is its own problem

Task initiation is the moment between knowing what to do and doing it. It is not a diagnosis. It is a description of a stuck point, and it is common enough that the CDC lists, among the struggles of adults with ADHD, managing attention, staying organized and completing lengthy tasks unless they are interesting. The techniques below all do the same thing: they make the first move smaller, more specific or less lonely.

Seven techniques

1. Shrink the task to a first step

The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) advice for adults is to break large tasks into smaller, more manageable steps. CHADD's list of strategies says the same. Keep shrinking until the step feels almost silly.

The smallest start is not the whole job. It is only a way in. Once you are moving, keep going if you can, and stop without guilt if you cannot.

2. Name the first physical action

"Work on taxes" is a project. "Find the folder labeled 2025" is an action. Write the first action as a verb you could do in under a minute, and put it where you will see it.

3. Make an if-then plan

An implementation intention is a plan in the form "If X happens, then I will do Y": "If I pour my coffee, then I open the document." In a 2006 meta-analysis of 94 tests, Gollwitzer and Sheeran found that if-then plans had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (d = 0.65). In a study by Gawrilow and Gollwitzer, children with ADHD who used if-then plans improved their performance on an inhibition task to the level of children without ADHD. That task was about stopping a response, not starting one, and it was in children, so treat it as a promising lead rather than proof for adult task initiation.

4. Set a timer for a short burst

CHADD lists timers, visual timers such as the Time Timer, and the Pomodoro Technique among tools for time blindness. A common pattern is 25 minutes of work and a short break. Agreeing to "just 10 minutes" makes starting cheaper.

5. Use another person

CHADD describes body doubling as a tool some adults use to start and complete projects: a friend or partner works at the same time, in person or by video. It says this forces you to choose a specific project, set aside a specific time and be accountable to another person, and that there has not been significant research on how well it works. It costs nothing to try.

6. Get it out of your head

NIMH recommends writing down assignments, messages, appointments, reminders and important thoughts. For starting, add a reminder that names the first step rather than the project. Preparing everything the night before and keeping a checklist by the door are both on CHADD's list.

7. Lower the bar on hard days

Decide in advance what a short version looks like: one step instead of five. Doing the short version beats waiting for a better day.

When to talk to someone

The NIMH says medication and therapy are the most effective treatments for ADHD, and that a health care provider is a good place to start. The CDC says the first step is to talk with a healthcare provider about whether your symptoms fit ADHD. If trouble starting tasks is hurting your work, school or relationships, ask for an evaluation instead of trying to out-organize it alone.

Frequently asked questions

Why can't I start tasks when I have ADHD?

Task initiation is the gap between knowing what to do and beginning it. The CDC lists trouble managing attention, staying organized and finishing lengthy tasks unless they are interesting among adult ADHD struggles. Trouble starting is not a separate diagnosis.

What is the fastest way to start a task you are avoiding?

Shrink the first step until it is almost silly, such as opening the document or putting on one sock. NIMH advises breaking large tasks into smaller, more manageable steps.

Does body doubling work for ADHD?

CHADD describes it as a tool some adults use to start and complete projects, and says there has not been significant research to validate how it works. It is free and low risk to try.

What is an if-then plan?

It is a plan in the form 'If X happens, then I will do Y,' such as 'If I pour my coffee, then I open the document.' A 2006 meta-analysis of 94 tests found a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals.

Can an app help with ADHD task initiation?

An app can lower the cost of starting by showing one step at a time and offering a smaller start. Next Cue does this with its I'm stuck button, but it is a planning aid and does not treat ADHD.
